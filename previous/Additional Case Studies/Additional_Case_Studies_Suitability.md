# Additional Case Studies — Suitability Across All Three Seminar Days

This document explains how the 10 additional case studies (defined in
`Additional_Case_Studies.md`) map onto the three-day structure of the
*AI-Assisted Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis* seminar. It answers the question:
**can a corpus built in the corpus-construction session be carried through to screening
and then to evidence synthesis?**

The short answer is **yes for all 10 in Days 1 and 2, and yes for six of the ten in
Day 3 as a quantitative meta-analysis** — with the remaining four working well as
narrative or scoping synthesis examples.

---

## What Each Day Needs from a Case Study

| Day | What it does | What it needs from the corpus |
|---|---|---|
| **Day 1 — From Query to Corpus** | Boolean query → open API → deduplicated corpus | A well-formed Boolean query, a suitable API, and a corpus of ~50–200 records with titles, abstracts, years, and DOIs |
| **Day 2 — From Corpus to Included Studies** | AI-assisted screening → inclusion/exclusion decisions | The same corpus **plus** abstracts rich enough for the screener to apply inclusion/exclusion criteria, and a clearly defined PICO or SPIDER framework |
| **Day 3 — From Included Studies to Evidence** | Data extraction → evidence synthesis / meta-analysis | A subset of included studies **plus** extractable effect sizes, outcomes, or structured data — this is where the case study either supports a quantitative pooling or requires a narrative approach |

## Suitability of the 10 Case Studies Across All Three Days

| # | Topic | Works for Day 2 (Screening)? | Works for Day 3 (Synthesis)? | Day 3 synthesis type | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Technology-enhanced learning & academic achievement | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes | Quantitative meta-analysis | Clear PICO; many RCTs with standardised mean differences (SMD) for achievement scores |
| 2 | Mindfulness-based interventions & anxiety | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes | Quantitative meta-analysis | Ideal — well-defined outcome (validated anxiety scales), large RCT base, consistent effect measure |
| 3 | Carbon pricing & emissions reduction | ✅ Yes | ⚠️ Partial | Narrative synthesis | Heterogeneous outcomes (% reduction, absolute tonnes, GDP share) make pooling methodologically contested; narrative synthesis is more appropriate |
| 4 | LLMs in clinical decision support | ✅ Yes | ⚠️ Partial | Scoping / narrative synthesis | Literature is too new and methodologically heterogeneous for meta-analysis; excellent for demonstrating a scoping review synthesis |
| 5 | Microfinance & household poverty reduction | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes | Quantitative meta-analysis | Many RCTs with income and consumption outcomes; a classic meta-analysis candidate with well-documented effect sizes |
| 6 | Nurse staffing ratios & patient safety | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes | Quantitative meta-analysis | Well-defined outcome (in-hospital mortality rate); large observational literature with extractable odds ratios and risk ratios |
| 7 | Social media & political polarisation | ✅ Yes | ⚠️ Partial | Narrative / vote-counting synthesis | Outcomes are measured very differently across studies (survey scales, experimental tasks, social network metrics); vote-counting or narrative synthesis is more realistic |
| 8 | Green infrastructure & urban heat island | ✅ Yes | ⚠️ Partial | Quantitative synthesis (with caveats) | Outcomes (temperature reduction in °C) are extractable but study designs are heterogeneous; a meta-analysis is possible but requires careful subgroup analysis |
| 9 | Statin therapy & cardiovascular events | ✅ Yes | ✅ Yes | Quantitative meta-analysis | The gold-standard meta-analysis topic — binary outcome (cardiovascular event: yes/no), large RCT base, well-defined effect measure (RR or OR) |
| 10 | Open access publishing & citation advantage | ✅ Yes | ⚠️ Partial | Narrative / bibliometric synthesis | Observational bibliometric studies; pooling citation ratios is methodologically contested; better suited to a descriptive or narrative synthesis |

## Pedagogical Notes

### Cases best suited to quantitative meta-analysis on Day 3 (Cases 1, 2, 5, 6, 9)

These five cases have three properties that make them ideal for the Day 3 meta-analysis
workflow: (a) a clearly defined, consistently measured outcome; (b) a sufficient number
of primary studies using comparable designs; and (c) extractable effect sizes (SMD, RR,
OR, or mean difference) that can be pooled in a forest plot. Case 9 (statin therapy) is
particularly recommended for demonstrating the full pipeline because it has the largest
and most methodologically uniform evidence base.

### Cases best suited to narrative or scoping synthesis on Day 3 (Cases 3, 4, 7, 10)

These four cases are not weaknesses — they are pedagogically valuable precisely because
they illustrate a critical lesson: **not every corpus leads to a forest plot**. Many
real-world systematic reviews conclude with a narrative synthesis because the primary
literature is too heterogeneous for quantitative pooling. Using these cases on Day 3
gives participants practice in recognising when meta-analysis is and is not appropriate,
and in applying structured narrative synthesis frameworks (e.g., SWiM — Synthesis Without
Meta-analysis).

### Case 8 (Green infrastructure) — a borderline case

Case 8 sits between the two groups. A meta-analysis is technically possible if the corpus
is restricted to studies that measure surface temperature reduction in degrees Celsius
using comparable measurement protocols. This makes it a useful teaching case for
demonstrating how subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis can rescue a heterogeneous
corpus for quantitative synthesis.

| Day | Recommended cases for live demonstration | Recommended cases for participant practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Case 4 (LLMs — fast-moving, dramatic year distribution) | Any of the 10 |
